Condition 01

Heart attack

A heart attack occurs when the blood supply to part of the heart muscle is suddenly blocked or greatly reduced. This is a medical emergency.

What causes it?

The heart muscle needs its own supply of oxygen-rich blood. It gets this through the coronary arteries. Fatty deposits called plaques can build up in these arteries. If a plaque ruptures, a blood clot may form and block blood flow.

What happens to the body?

  1. Less oxygen reaches part of the heart muscle.
  2. Muscle cells cannot carry out enough aerobic respiration to meet their energy needs.
  3. Cells become damaged and may die if blood flow is not restored quickly.
  4. The heart may pump less effectively or develop a dangerous abnormal rhythm.

Possible symptoms

  • Chest pressure, tightness, squeezing or pain
  • Pain spreading to an arm, shoulder, back, neck or jaw
  • Shortness of breath, cold sweat, nausea or dizziness
  • Some people have mild or less typical symptoms, or no obvious chest pain
Important: A heart attack is different from a cardiac arrest. A heart attack is a problem with blood supply to the heart muscle; a cardiac arrest is when the heart suddenly stops pumping blood effectively. A heart attack can lead to cardiac arrest. In New Zealand, call 111 for suspected heart attack symptoms.

What may help?

Emergency treatment aims to restore blood flow, sometimes using a balloon and stent. Risk can be reduced through habits such as not smoking, being physically active and managing blood pressure and cholesterol.

Why is a coronary artery blockage dangerous?

It prevents enough oxygen-rich blood from reaching heart muscle cells. Without oxygen, these cells cannot meet energy demands through aerobic respiration, and the tissue can become damaged or die.

Does a heart attack mean the heart has stopped?

Not necessarily. A heart attack is a blood-supply problem. The heart may keep beating, although a heart attack can trigger cardiac arrest.

Condition 02

Frostbite

Frostbite is an injury in which skin and sometimes deeper tissues freeze after exposure to extreme cold. Fingers, toes, ears, cheeks and the nose are especially vulnerable.

What causes it?

When we are cold, small blood vessels near the skin constrict (become narrower). This reduces heat loss and helps keep vital organs warm. In extreme cold, prolonged reduced blood flow and the freezing of tissue can injure cells.

What happens to the body?

  1. Blood vessels near the skin narrow, so less warm blood reaches the area.
  2. Extreme cold can cause ice crystals to form in tissues.
  3. Cells and blood vessels are injured; blood flow can be impaired further.
  4. Severe frostbite can damage or kill tissue, sometimes permanently.

Possible symptoms

  • Cold skin with tingling, pain or stinging at first
  • Numbness or loss of feeling
  • Skin that looks pale, white, greyish, yellowish or unusually dark, depending on skin tone and severity
  • Skin that feels firm or waxy; blisters may appear as injury develops
  • Severe cases can lead to dead tissue and lasting loss of function
Important: Frostbite is not simply “blood stopping”. It involves freezing injury as well as reduced circulation. Suspected frostbite needs prompt medical advice. Get out of the cold; do not rub the area or use direct intense heat. Avoid rewarming if it could freeze again before reaching safety.

What may help?

Prevent it by staying dry and covering exposed skin with suitable warm clothing. Medical treatment involves safe rewarming and assessment of tissue damage.

Check your understanding

Is narrowing of blood vessels always a bad thing?

No. In cold conditions, vasoconstriction can reduce heat loss and protect core body temperature. But extreme and prolonged cold can reduce tissue blood supply and, importantly, freeze and injure tissues.

Why can severe frostbite cause permanent damage?

Freezing and associated damage to cells and small blood vessels can lead to tissue death. Severe injury can affect nerves, muscles and other deeper tissues.
