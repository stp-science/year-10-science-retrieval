Think back to our human body topic

Blood delivers oxygen and glucose to cells. Cells release energy by aerobic respiration. Heart muscle cells use this energy to contract and pump blood. What happens if their oxygen supply is suddenly interrupted?

Understand the problem

What causes a heart attack?

A heart attack (myocardial infarction) usually happens when blood flow through a coronary artery is blocked. Coronary arteries supply the heart muscle itself with oxygen-rich blood.

The most common cause

  1. Over time, fatty deposits called plaques build up in coronary arteries.
  2. A plaque may rupture (break open).
  3. A blood clot forms at the damaged plaque.
  4. The clot can block the artery and stop oxygen-rich blood reaching part of the heart muscle.

Some heart attacks have other causes, but this is the main pathway to understand at Year 10.

Symptoms

  • Chest pressure, squeezing, heaviness or pain
  • Pain or discomfort spreading to an arm, back, jaw or neck
  • Shortness of breath, sweating, nausea or light-headedness
  • Symptoms can vary: some people do not have obvious chest pain
Medical emergency: If a heart attack is suspected, call 111 in New Zealand. Do not wait for every symptom to appear.

What happens to the body?

Blocked coronary artery→Less oxygen delivered→Less energy produced by aerobic respiration→Heart muscle cells become damaged

Explain the science

The heart needs a constant supply of energy to keep beating. When blood flow is blocked, heart muscle cells receive less oxygen. They cannot produce enough energy through aerobic respiration to meet their needs. If blood flow is not restored, some cells die and the heart may pump less effectively.

Possible consequences

  • Damaged heart muscle: some muscle may no longer contract normally.
  • Reduced pumping: the heart may struggle to deliver enough blood to the body.
  • Abnormal heartbeat: an interruption in the electrical rhythm can be dangerous.
  • Heart failure: severe injury can reduce pumping ability in the longer term.
Important distinction: A heart attack is a problem with the blood supply to heart muscle. A cardiac arrest means the heart suddenly stops pumping blood effectively. A heart attack can cause a cardiac arrest, but they are not the same thing.

Test your explanation

Why do heart muscle cells need oxygen?

Oxygen is needed for aerobic respiration, which releases energy used for muscle contraction.

Why can a heart attack affect blood flow to the rest of the body?

Because damaged heart muscle may not contract and pump as effectively, so less blood may circulate around the body.

Restoring blood flow

How are heart attacks treated?

Treatment aims to restore blood flow quickly, limit damage to heart muscle and prevent further clots or future heart attacks. Doctors choose treatments based on the type of heart attack and the person's condition.

1. Angioplasty and stents

A thin tube called a catheter is guided into a coronary artery. A small balloon is inflated to widen the blocked section. Often, a stent — a tiny metal mesh tube — is left in place to help keep the artery open.

Why it works: more oxygen-rich blood can reach heart muscle cells again, limiting further damage.

Important: A stent holds an artery open. It does not replace the heart, and it is not a medicine.

2. Medicines

Antiplatelet medicines (e.g. aspirin)

Make platelets less likely to clump together, reducing the chance of harmful blood clots.

Clot-dissolving medicines (thrombolytics)

In some heart attacks, doctors use these to break down a clot if rapid angioplasty is not available or suitable.

Statins

Lower LDL cholesterol and help reduce the risk of further plaque-related problems.

Beta-blockers and other medicines

Some reduce the heart's workload or blood pressure and help protect the heart during recovery.

3. Coronary artery bypass surgery

In some situations, surgeons use another blood vessel to create a new route for blood to travel around a blocked artery.

4. Recovery and reducing future risk

People may be offered cardiac rehabilitation, follow-up appointments, prescribed medicines and support to stop smoking, exercise safely and manage cholesterol or blood pressure.

Safety: These are explanations of medical treatments, not instructions for self-treatment. Call 111 for suspected heart attack symptoms and follow emergency service advice.

Challenge question

Why might a patient need medicines even after a stent has reopened their coronary artery?

Reveal suggested explanation

A stent helps reopen one narrowed or blocked area, but it does not remove the underlying risk of future clots and plaque-related disease. Medicines can reduce clotting and other risk factors.
