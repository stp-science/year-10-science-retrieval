How the systems connect

Digestion releases glucose from food. Glucose is absorbed into the blood. Gas exchange adds oxygen to the blood. The circulatory system delivers both to cells. Cells use them for aerobic respiration, releasing energy for life processes.

01 / Cellular processes

Aerobic respiration

A chemical reaction in living cells that releases energy from glucose using oxygen.

Learn these facts

glucose + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water
Energy is released for cells to use
  • Reactants: glucose and oxygen. Products: carbon dioxide and water.
  • Most aerobic respiration happens in the mitochondria of cells.
  • The energy released is used for movement, growth, repair, temperature control and other life processes.
  • Respiration is not the movement of air: it is a chemical process inside cells.
  • Glucose is supplied by digestion and absorption; oxygen is supplied by gas exchange. The blood transports both.
Exam tip: If you are asked to explain why cells need oxygen, link it to aerobic respiration and the release of energy.

02 / Getting nutrients

Digestive system

Digestion breaks large, insoluble food molecules into smaller molecules that can be absorbed.

The journey of food

  • Mouth: teeth break food into smaller pieces; saliva begins chemical digestion.
  • Oesophagus: pushes food towards the stomach by peristalsis (muscle contractions).
  • Stomach: churns food and begins protein digestion using enzymes and acid.
  • Small intestine: digestion finishes and soluble nutrients are absorbed into the blood.
  • Pancreas: makes digestive enzymes, released into the small intestine.
  • Liver: makes bile to help break fat into tiny droplets for enzyme action.
  • Large intestine: absorbs water. Rectum: stores faeces. Anus: faeces leave the body.

Physical versus chemical digestion

Physical digestion

Chewing and churning break food into smaller pieces, increasing its surface area without changing the food molecules chemically.

Chemical digestion

Enzymes break large molecules into smaller, soluble molecules that can be absorbed. Enzymes are biological catalysts.

Examples: Carbohydrases break down carbohydrates into sugars; proteases break proteins into amino acids; lipases break fats into fatty acids and glycerol.

03 / Food and health

Nutrients & diabetes

Different food molecules have different jobs. Blood glucose regulation is an important part of keeping the body healthy.

Food groups to recognise

Carbohydrates

Bread, rice, potatoes, fruit. Digested to sugars such as glucose, used in respiration.

Proteins

Eggs, fish, beans. Provide amino acids for growth and repair.

Lipids (fats)

Oils, nuts, butter. Concentrated energy store and cell structures.

Vitamins & minerals

Fruit, vegetables, dairy and other foods. Needed in small amounts for healthy body functions.

Fibre

Wholegrains, vegetables. Helps food travel through the gut.

Water

Needed for transport and chemical reactions throughout the body.

What is diabetes?

Diabetes is a condition in which blood glucose becomes too high because the body does not make enough insulin or does not respond properly to it. Insulin is a hormone produced by the pancreas that helps regulate blood glucose.

Type 1

The immune system attacks insulin-producing cells. The body makes little or no insulin. It is not caused by eating sugar. Insulin treatment is needed.

Type 2

Body cells respond less effectively to insulin and the body may not make enough. Risk involves several factors, including genetics, activity and diet.

Important distinction: Eating sugar does not by itself mean someone will develop type 2 diabetes. A diet regularly high in added sugar can contribute to excess energy intake, but diabetes risk has several causes.

04 / Getting glucose into the blood

Absorption & villi

The small intestine is specially adapted to absorb digested nutrients efficiently.

The structure of a villus

  • Absorption: movement of digested nutrients through the gut wall into the blood (many fats enter lymph first).
  • Villi and microvilli create a very large surface area.
  • A thin outer wall gives a short diffusion distance.
  • A rich capillary supply carries nutrients away and helps maintain a concentration difference.
  • Diffusion: net movement of particles from higher to lower concentration.
Answer pattern: Name the feature → say how it works → explain why more nutrient is absorbed in a given time.
Check your understanding

A student says, “Villi are useful because they are small.” Improve the answer using two specific adaptations.

Show suggested answerVilli have a large surface area for more nutrients to cross at once. Their thin walls shorten the distance for diffusion.
05 / Getting oxygen into the blood

Gas exchange

Oxygen diffuses from alveoli into capillary blood; carbon dioxide diffuses in the opposite direction.

Structure and function

  • Lungs: contain millions of alveoli.
  • Bronchi: two main branches carrying air into the lungs. Bronchioles: smaller branches leading to alveoli.
  • Alveoli: tiny air sacs where gases diffuse between air and surrounding blood capillaries.
  • Oxygen: moves from higher concentration in alveolar air into lower-concentration blood.
  • Carbon dioxide: moves from higher concentration in incoming blood into alveolar air.

Why alveoli are effective

  • Millions of alveoli provide a large surface area.
  • Alveolar and capillary walls are one cell thick, giving a short diffusion distance.
  • A dense network of capillaries helps maintain concentration gradients.

06 / Transporting substances

Circulation & the heart

The heart pumps blood around the body so cells receive oxygen and nutrients and waste products can be removed.

Blood vessels

Arteries

Carry blood away from the heart, usually under high pressure. Thick muscular, elastic walls.

Veins

Carry blood towards the heart, under lower pressure. Have valves to help prevent backflow.

Capillaries

Very narrow vessels with thin walls for the exchange of oxygen, nutrients and wastes by diffusion.

Blood

Transports oxygen, glucose and other nutrients, carbon dioxide and other materials around the body.

Remember: arteries are not always oxygenated; the pulmonary artery carries deoxygenated blood to the lungs.

Pathway through the heart

  • Right side: receives deoxygenated blood from the body and pumps it to the lungs.
  • Left side: receives oxygenated blood from the lungs and pumps it to the body.
  • Valves help blood travel in one direction.
  • Left ventricle has a thicker muscular wall because it pumps blood around the whole body at higher pressure.
Exam tip: Say away from or towards the heart when identifying arteries and veins. Oxygen content alone is not enough.

07 / Putting it all together

Heart health & body systems

A blocked coronary artery can stop heart muscle getting the oxygen and glucose it needs for aerobic respiration.

Heart attacks

  • Coronary arteries supply oxygenated blood to heart muscle.
  • Fatty plaques can narrow the arteries. A clot can block an artery.
  • Reduced oxygen supply means heart muscle cells cannot carry out sufficient aerobic respiration. Cells may be damaged or die.
  • Possible symptoms include chest pressure or pain, pain spreading to an arm/jaw, sweating, nausea or unusual shortness of breath. Symptoms vary.
  • Medical treatment can include medicines and angioplasty with a stent to open a blocked artery. Treatment is time-critical.
First aid: In New Zealand, call 111 immediately if you suspect a heart attack. Follow the emergency operator's instructions. Do not delay seeking help. This is general safety guidance, not a substitute for first-aid training.

Explain the whole system

Try this 6-mark explanation framework:

  1. Food carbohydrates are digested into glucose.
  2. Glucose is absorbed through the villi into blood.
  3. Oxygen moves from the alveoli into blood by diffusion.
  4. The heart pumps blood through vessels to tissues.
  5. Glucose and oxygen reach cells and are used in aerobic respiration.
  6. Energy is released for contraction, growth, repair and other cell functions; carbon dioxide is carried away.
